How the AI Impact Score WorksAI DANGER LEVEL:
HOW IT'S SCORED

Every career in this tool carries a 0–100 AI impact score. This page explains exactly what that number means, how it was assigned, and — just as importantly — what it can't tell you.

EVERY CAREER HAS A 0-100 AI DANGER SCORE.
HERE'S WHAT IT MEANS AND WHERE IT COMES FROM.

Read this firstREAD THIS FIRST

These scores are informed judgment calls, not measurements.THESE SCORES ARE JUDGMENT CALLS, NOT MEASUREMENTS.

There is no official government or academic dataset that publishes an "AI impact score" per occupation. The scores in this tool were assigned by applying a consistent rubric (described below) to each occupation's task profile. Two thoughtful analysts using the same rubric could easily land 10–15 points apart on any given career.

NO OFFICIAL DATASET PUBLISHES AN "AI IMPACT SCORE" PER JOB. THESE WERE ASSIGNED BY APPLYING A RUBRIC TO EACH JOB'S TASKS. TWO ANALYSTS COULD EASILY DIFFER BY 10-15 POINTS.

Treat the score as a conversation starter and a way to compare careers relative to each other — not as a prediction about your future.

USE IT TO COMPARE CAREERS AGAINST EACH OTHER. NOT AS A PREDICTION ABOUT YOUR FUTURE.

What the score actually measuresWHAT THE SCORE MEASURES

The score estimates task exposure — the share of an occupation's day-to-day work that current and near-term AI systems could plausibly perform, assist with, or make dramatically faster.

THE SCORE ESTIMATES TASK EXPOSURE — HOW MUCH OF THE JOB'S DAILY WORK AI COULD DO, ASSIST WITH, OR SPEED UP.

A critical distinction that trips people up:

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION:

  • Exposure is not elimination. A high score means AI touches a lot of the work. It does not mean the job disappears. Radiology is heavily AI-exposed and radiologist demand is still growing.
  • EXPOSURE ≠ ELIMINATION. A HIGH SCORE MEANS AI TOUCHES A LOT OF THE WORK. NOT THAT THE JOB VANISHES.
  • Exposure often means transformation. The most common outcome is that the role's task mix shifts — routine work shrinks, judgment and client work expand.
  • EXPOSURE USUALLY MEANS TRANSFORMATION. ROUTINE WORK SHRINKS, JUDGMENT WORK GROWS.
  • A low score isn't automatically good news. Some AI-resilient jobs are resilient because they're physical and low-paid, not because they're valuable and protected.
  • LOW SCORE ISN'T AUTO-GOOD. SOME SAFE JOBS ARE SAFE BECAUSE THEY'RE PHYSICAL AND LOW-PAID.

The five-dimension rubricTHE 5-DIMENSION RUBRIC

Each occupation was assessed on five dimensions. Scores start at a baseline of 50 and move up or down based on how the occupation profiles on each.

EACH JOB IS RATED ON 5 DIMENSIONS. START AT 50, THEN ADJUST.

1. Task routineness1. ROUTINENESS

Pushes score upPUSHES SCORE UP

How much of the work follows repeatable, rule-based patterns? Document review, data entry, standardized reporting, and template-driven writing score high here.

HOW MUCH WORK FOLLOWS REPEATABLE RULES? DATA ENTRY, DOC REVIEW, TEMPLATE WRITING SCORE HIGH.

2. Data-centricity2. DATA-CENTRIC

Pushes score upPUSHES SCORE UP

Does the job primarily process structured text, numbers, or images? Language models and vision models are strongest exactly where the raw material is digital.

DOES THE JOB PROCESS TEXT, NUMBERS, OR IMAGES? AI IS STRONGEST WHERE INPUT IS DIGITAL.

3. Physical & in-person demand3. PHYSICAL / IN-PERSON

Pushes score downPUSHES SCORE DOWN

Hands-on work, clinical contact, lab procedures, and physical presence are expensive and slow to automate. Robotics lags software by a wide margin.

HANDS-ON WORK, CLINICAL CONTACT, LAB WORK. ROBOTICS LAGS SOFTWARE BADLY.

4. Accountability & trust4. ACCOUNTABILITY

Pushes score downPUSHES SCORE DOWN

Does someone need to sign, certify, or be legally liable? Licensure, fiduciary duty, and regulatory sign-off create durable human requirements regardless of capability.

DOES SOMEONE NEED TO SIGN OR BE LIABLE? LICENSES AND REGULATION PROTECT ROLES.

5. Complex human interaction5. HUMAN INTERACTION

Pushes score downPUSHES SCORE DOWN

Negotiation, persuasion, counseling, teaching, and managing conflict. High-stakes interpersonal work where people specifically want a human counterpart.

NEGOTIATION, COUNSELING, TEACHING, CONFLICT. PEOPLE WANT A HUMAN HERE.

6. AI as amplifier6. AI AS AMPLIFIER

Pushes score downPUSHES SCORE DOWN

Some roles get more valuable as AI spreads — the people who build, deploy, audit, and govern these systems. Demand rises with adoption rather than falling.

SOME ROLES GET MORE VALUABLE AS AI SPREADS — THE PEOPLE WHO BUILD AND GOVERN IT.

What informed the rubricWHAT INFORMED THE RUBRIC

The dimensions above draw on a body of published research on occupational automation exposure. None of these sources produced the specific numbers in this tool, but they shaped how the dimensions were chosen and weighted:

THE DIMENSIONS DRAW ON PUBLISHED RESEARCH. NONE OF THESE PRODUCED OUR EXACT NUMBERS, BUT THEY SHAPED THE APPROACH:

  • O*NET task and work-activity data — the U.S. Department of Labor's occupational database, which decomposes jobs into constituent tasks. The starting point for any task-level analysis.
  • O*NET TASK DATA — DOL DATABASE BREAKING JOBS INTO TASKS.
  • Frey & Osborne (2013), "The Future of Employment" — the paper that popularized occupation-level automation probability. Widely critiqued for treating whole occupations as automatable rather than individual tasks, which is why this rubric works at the task level instead.
  • FREY & OSBORNE (2013) — POPULARIZED AUTOMATION PROBABILITY. CRITIQUED FOR WHOLE-JOB SCORING.
  • Eloundou et al. (2023), "GPTs are GPTs" — task-level exposure analysis specifically for large language models, which is the technology most relevant to the white-collar careers in this dataset.
  • ELOUNDOU ET AL. (2023) — TASK-LEVEL EXPOSURE FOR LANGUAGE MODELS.
  • Felten, Raj & Seamans — the AI Occupational Exposure index, which maps AI capability advances onto occupational abilities.
  • FELTEN, RAJ & SEAMANS — AI OCCUPATIONAL EXPOSURE INDEX.
  • BLS Employment Projections (2023–2033) — used as a sanity check. When a projection strongly contradicted a score, the score was revisited.
  • BLS PROJECTIONS 2023-2033 — USED AS A SANITY CHECK.

If you want to dig into the underlying occupational data yourself, every career card links directly to its BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook entry.

EVERY CAREER CARD LINKS TO ITS BLS PAGE IF YOU WANT THE RAW DATA.

Known limitationsKNOWN LIMITATIONS

  • Single-assessor bias. These scores reflect one consistent rubric applied by one source. No panel, no inter-rater reliability check, no confidence intervals.
  • SINGLE-ASSESSOR BIAS. ONE RUBRIC, ONE SOURCE. NO PANEL, NO ERROR BARS.
  • Snapshot in time. AI capability moves fast. A score written today may be badly wrong within two years, in either direction.
  • SNAPSHOT IN TIME. AI MOVES FAST. THESE MAY BE WRONG IN 2 YEARS.
  • Capability ≠ adoption. Technical feasibility says nothing about how fast employers actually change. Regulation, capital costs, union agreements, and institutional inertia all slow diffusion — sometimes by decades.
  • CAPABILITY ≠ ADOPTION. WHAT'S POSSIBLE ISN'T WHAT EMPLOYERS DO. REGULATION AND INERTIA SLOW THINGS DOWN.
  • Occupation-level, not job-level. Two accountants at different firms can face completely different exposure. Your specific employer, specialty, and seniority matter more than the occupational average.
  • JOB TITLE ≠ YOUR JOB. TWO ACCOUNTANTS AT DIFFERENT FIRMS FACE DIFFERENT EXPOSURE.
  • Ignores new job creation. The rubric measures pressure on existing tasks. It cannot anticipate roles that don't exist yet — and historically, those have been substantial.
  • IGNORES NEW JOBS. CAN'T PREDICT ROLES THAT DON'T EXIST YET.
  • No salary or geography adjustment. Exposure is scored independently of pay or location, though both meaningfully affect real-world outcomes.
  • NO PAY OR LOCATION ADJUSTMENT.

The Career Breadth scoreSKILL TREE SCORE

Each major also carries a 0–100 Career Breadth score estimating how widely its graduates spread across different occupations. Applied Mathematics (90) opens doors in banking, tech, government, and research; Actuarial Mathematics (20) points almost exclusively at one profession. Like the AI score, this is an informed judgment call — anchored to two public data sources, but not computed from them.

EACH MAJOR HAS A 0–100 SKILL TREE SCORE: HOW MANY DIFFERENT JOBS ITS GRADS ACTUALLY GET. APPLIED MATH (90) = HUGE TREE. ACTUARIAL MATH (20) = ONE DEEP BRANCH.

Two inputs informed the ratings:

TWO INPUTS:

  • The NCES CIP→SOC crosswalk — the official government mapping from fields of study to the occupations they prepare students for. More mapped occupations suggests more doors.
  • NCES CIP→SOC CROSSWALK. OFFICIAL MAP FROM MAJORS TO JOBS. MORE JOBS = BIGGER TREE.
  • Census ACS field-of-degree data — what working adults with each degree actually do, which captures outcome concentration: the share of grads who end up in the single most common occupation.
  • CENSUS ACS DATA. WHAT GRADS ACTUALLY DO AT AGE 30, NOT WHAT'S THEORETICALLY POSSIBLE.

Focused is not bad. Broad is not automatically good.FOCUSED ≠ BAD. BROAD ≠ AUTO-GOOD.

A focused major usually means a legible pipeline: recruiters come to campus, the credential path is marked, and placement is near-certain — Actuarial Mathematics' 100% employment rate is the payoff of its narrowness. Breadth means freedom, but also the burden of charting your own route. If you know exactly what you want, focus is a feature. If you're undecided, breadth is insurance.

A FOCUSED MAJOR = A MARKED QUEST LINE. RECRUITERS COME TO YOU. A BIG TREE = FREEDOM, BUT YOU CHART YOUR OWN ROUTE. KNOW WHAT YOU WANT? FOCUS IS A FEATURE. UNDECIDED? BREADTH IS INSURANCE.

Breadth also interacts with the AI score: our AI ratings are judgment calls that could be wrong by 10–15 points, and a narrow major is a concentrated bet on the rating for one occupation being right. The riskiest combination in this dataset is focused breadth + high AI exposure — a compounding bet.

COMBO WARNING: SMALL SKILL TREE + HIGH AI DANGER = ALL EGGS IN ONE EXPOSED BASKET.

Combining majors. The Combine view merges two majors' career paths (deduplicated) and reports the union's facts directly: best-case AI resilience, bachelor's-reachable paths, salaries. The one synthesized number is combined breadth, computed as: the higher of the two breadth scores, +10 if the majors sit in different colleges, +10 scaled by how little their underlying BLS occupations overlap, capped at 100. That is a deliberately simple heuristic, not a measurement — redundant pairs (like Marketing + Digital Marketing) get almost no uplift, complementary ones get more. A minor moves you in the same direction as a second major, with a smaller effect.

FUSION MATH: CO-OP MODE MERGES TWO SKILL TREES. FUSION BONUS = +10 CROSS-WORLD, +10 FOR LOW OVERLAP, CAP 100. A HEURISTIC, NOT A MEASUREMENT. MINOR = SAME BUFF, SMALLER.

The payback estimateGRIND TIME MATH

Each career shows an estimated payback period: how long it would take to recoup the cost of a Bryant degree. The formula is deliberately simple and fully adjustable:

EACH CAREER SHOWS GRIND TIME: HOW LONG TO EARN BACK YOUR COIN. SIMPLE FORMULA, YOU SET THE COST:

Payback years = (4 × your annual net cost) ÷ (15% of the career's entry salary)

GRIND YRS = (4 × YEARLY COIN COST) ÷ (15% × LVL 1 SALARY)

  • Your cost, not one cost. The slider defaults to Bryant's average net price (~$47,200/year for 2025–26, after the average ~$27K in aid; full sticker with room and board is ~$71,600). Your aid letter is the real number — set the slider to it.
  • SET YOUR OWN COST. DEFAULT = AVG NET PRICE (~$47.2K/YR). STICKER = ~$71.6K. YOUR AID LETTER IS THE TRUTH.
  • Why 15%? It mirrors typical income-driven student-loan repayment. It is an assumption, not advice — repaying faster or slower changes the years, not the ranking.
  • WHY 15%? TYPICAL LOAN REPAYMENT RATE. CHANGES THE YEARS, NOT THE RANKING.
  • Entry salary, on purpose. New grads earn entry wages, so the estimate is conservative. It ignores raises, which shorten real payback.
  • USES LVL 1 SALARY. CONSERVATIVE. RAISES SHORTEN REAL GRIND TIME.
  • Grad-school paths are not modeled. Careers with the 🎓 badge need more school — more cost and more years — that this estimate does not include. Medical school alone can add $250K+.
  • 🎓 PATHS NOT MODELED. GRAD QUESTS COST EXTRA COIN + YEARS. NOT IN THIS MATH.
  • National medians, not Bryant outcomes. Salaries are BLS national figures, not what Bryant grads specifically earn. Treat payback as the shape of the tradeoff, not a personal forecast.
  • NATIONAL DATA, NOT BRYANT-SPECIFIC. SHAPE OF THE TRADEOFF, NOT YOUR FORTUNE.

How to actually use thisHOW TO USE THIS

  1. Compare, don't predict. The gap between a 25 and a 70 is meaningful. The gap between a 48 and a 52 is noise.
  2. COMPARE, DON'T PREDICT. 25 vs 70 MATTERS. 48 vs 52 IS NOISE.
  3. Read the analysis, not just the number. Each career includes a written assessment explaining which tasks are exposed and which are protected. That's where the useful information is.
  4. READ THE ANALYSIS, NOT JUST THE NUMBER. THE TEXT EXPLAINS WHICH TASKS ARE EXPOSED.
  5. Weigh it last, not first. Interest, aptitude, and genuine engagement predict career success far more reliably than any exposure metric. A motivated student in a high-exposure field will outperform a disengaged one in a "safe" field.
  6. WEIGH IT LAST. INTEREST AND APTITUDE PREDICT SUCCESS BETTER THAN ANY EXPOSURE SCORE.
  7. Use it to shape electives, not to rule out majors. The most productive response to a high score is usually adding complementary skills — not abandoning a field you're good at.
  8. USE IT FOR ELECTIVES, NOT TO RULE OUT MAJORS. ADD SKILLS INSTEAD OF QUITTING A FIELD YOU'RE GOOD AT.
  9. Talk to a human. Bryant's Amica Center for Career Education, faculty in your prospective department, and alumni working in the field will all know things this tool doesn't.
  10. TALK TO A HUMAN. CAREER SERVICES, FACULTY, AND ALUMNI KNOW THINGS THIS TOOL DOESN'T.
